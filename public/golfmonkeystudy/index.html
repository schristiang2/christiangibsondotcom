golf monkey

A Charleston golf brand with a great personality and a website that got in its way. This is the research behind the redesign, and the reason for every change.

client
Golf Monkey, Charleston, SC
role
Research, UX, visual design, front end
timeline
October 2026
status
Concept, presented to the owner

the starting point

The idea was strong. The site made strangers work to find it.

Golf Monkey is a golf company run by Bob Merryman, a club pro with 25 years in the Charleston area. It sells private lessons, an online coaching subscription called Monkey Wrench, merch, a podcast and a few community ideas still in the works.

The existing site, which carried many of the hallmarks of AI-generated work, turned all of that into a nine-hole golf course. Every offer became a "hole," a scorecard tracked which holes you had played, and each page ended with a teaser for the next tee. It was charming for about thirty seconds. After that, it was hard to tell what Golf Monkey actually did, or how to book a lesson.

research

First impressions happen fast, they're mostly visual, and AI tells cost trust.

Before changing anything, I looked at what's known about how people judge a site in the first few seconds, and at newer work on how people respond when something feels machine-made.

findingsourcewhat it meant for this project
People form an opinion of a page's look in about 50 milliseconds, and it holds after longer viewing.Lindgaard et al., Carleton University, 2006The first screen has to say what Golf Monkey is and what to do next.
46% of people judged a site's credibility partly on its overall design, the most-cited factor.Stanford Web Credibility study, 2,684 people, 2002An unfinished-looking site makes the business look unfinished.
Visually complex and unfamiliar layouts are rated less appealing at first glance. Simple, expected layouts win.Google Research, Tuch et al., 2012Cut decoration, and use navigation patterns people already know.
When readers suspected content was AI-made, trust fell by nearly half, whether or not it really was.Raptive, 3,000 US adults, 2025Removing the look of AI generation matters as much as removing actual mistakes.
When people notice AI in a brand's marketing, 31% trust it less and 7% trust it more.Klaviyo and Datalily, 8,000 consumers, 2025AI tells are four times more likely to hurt than help.

auditing the existing site

Next, I walked every page on desktop and phone and captured all 30 screens. Five patterns kept coming up.

no path

Nine equal "holes" meant nothing told a visitor where to start. Lessons, the business's main revenue, had the same weight as a tour that didn't exist yet.

a gimmick in charge

The golf course metaphor decided the structure, not the visitor's goal. It felt like a 1990s GeoCities site built for wandering, not for getting something done.

too much at once

Dotted lines, tiny monospace captions, par numbers and taglines on every row. Each was clever; together they were noise.

inside jokes up front

"Par 359," "Still pushing dirt," "Keep Mrs Pro happy." Funny to insiders, a foreign language to a first-time visitor.

doubled navigation

On desktop, a hamburger menu dropped down over a full row of hole links that already sat right below it.

pages from different sites

The holes, Monkey Wrench, Hall of Fame and the forfeit form each had a different header and layout, and nearly every hole ended with the same stamped-in video block. Both read as pages generated one prompt at a time.

principles

Four rules that every decision had to pass.

one clear path

People don't hang out on websites anymore. They come to get something. Every page should move a visitor toward booking, buying or listening.

familiar beats clever

Use layouts people already understand, and save the personality for the words, photos and clips.

one system, built for each job

Every page shares the same header, type and spacing. Each page's layout is shaped by what that page is for.

let the audience pick the jokes

Inside jokes are earned in the back-and-forth between a host and the audience. The site leaves room for that and doesn't front-load it.

decisions

What changed, and why.

01

say what it is on the first screen

The original home page opened on a hero photo and a scroll animation, and only then explained the business. The new first screen has a headline, one line about Bob, and two buttons: "Walk the course" and "Book a lesson — $125/hr." Merch, the course map and the podcast follow right after.

why

Visitors judge a page's look in about 50 milliseconds (Lindgaard). If the first screen doesn't state the offer, most people never scroll far enough to find it.

02

one menu, always visible

The hamburger menu and the duplicate row of hole links became a single header: the logo, every section in a visible row, and Pro Shop and Book a Lesson buttons in the corner.

why

Hamburger menus exist to save space on phones. On a wide screen they hide navigation that has room to be seen. A standard header is what people expect, and expected layouts win first impressions (Tuch et al.).

03

give booking its own page

Lessons pay the bills, but the price lived on the Lessons hole between two videos, and the button sent people off to a third-party scheduler. The new Book a Lesson page shows the three lesson types side by side, with a short form, the studio address and a number to text.

why

This is the one thing most visitors come to do, so it gets a page shaped around a single job. Nothing on it competes with the form.

04

show the whole shop at once

The merch boxes used to swipe past one at a time and shared their page with a podcast clip. The new Pro Shop shows all three boxes and all nine pieces in a grid, with prices, filters and a button on each.

why

A carousel hides most of its contents behind a gesture. A grid lets people compare at a glance, and comparing is what shoppers are there to do.

05

shape each page around its content

Bob's career used to be a sideways carousel tucked into a closing page. It now has its own page, The Pro, laid out as an actual golf scorecard with totals at the bottom: 25 years a club pro, 2 albatrosses, 4 holes-in-one, 1 quintuple bogey. University became a syllabus, and the Tour became a sign-up card.

why

The original stamped the same video block onto nearly every page, which reads as generated (Raptive). Giving each page a layout built for its own content makes the site feel made by a person, while a shared header and type system keeps it feeling like one site.

06

quiet the noise, keep the voice

I stripped out the decorative fairway drawings, par labels, dotted rules and most of the tiny taglines. Bob's quotes and video clips stayed, each one placed next to the copy it supports.

why

Visual complexity lowers first-glance appeal (Tuch et al.), and inside jokes on the first screen make newcomers feel left out. The personality now lives where it lands best: in Bob's own words.

what stayed

None of this replaced the brand. The tuxedo monkey, the midnight, fuchsia and powder-blue palette, the type from the brand brief, and every quote and clip carried over. A little golf survives in the page names and the "Next tee" link at the bottom of each page. It's just no longer the way you get around.

outcome

A third less to scroll, and a straight line to booking.

The redesign is a working concept, built and presented to the owner. It isn't live yet, so there's no traffic data. These are the measurable differences between the two builds.

what I'd measure after launch

  • Lesson requests per week, before and after.
  • Share of visitors who click Book a Lesson or Pro Shop from the home page.
  • Bounce rate on the home page from phones.

The bigger lesson: AI tools can get a site to "done" fast, but the result tends to follow a gimmick instead of a visitor. The fix is mostly editing: deciding what the page is for and removing everything that doesn't serve it.
