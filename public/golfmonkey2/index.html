Golf Monkey: What Changed and Why

Why I'm writing this

Bob, this follows The Case for a Redesign. That piece explained why the site needed to change. This one walks through every change I actually made, and the research behind each one, so you can see the thinking and push back on anything that doesn't sit right.

Three ideas drive almost everything here:

  • One page for each job. The site used to be one very long page. Now each thing Golf Monkey does gets its own page with its own button.
  • Personality in the voice, not the structure. The golf-course gimmick is gone. Your stories, quotes and jokes all stayed.
  • Community first. The podcast and the Shoe Bet are how Golf Monkey is building its following right now, so they moved to the top of the home page.

What I took out

Before redesigning anything, I went through the current site and pulled out what was hurting it. Some of it was broken, and some of it was quietly telling visitors the site wasn't finished.

One page for each job

The new site has ten pages: Home, Lessons, Monkey Wrench, Fitness, Podcast, Shop, Shoe Bet, About, and Tour and University as "coming soon" pages. Every page ends with the one thing a visitor came there to do: "Book a lesson" on Lessons, "Join the drop list" on Shop, "Sign the certificate" on the Shoe Bet, and so on.

Retiring the golf course

The first redesign kept a light version of the course theme: hole numbers, pars, a scorecard and a "Next tee" link on each page. Seeing it built out, it still felt like a GeoCities site. So the theme now lives in your voice, and the structure works like any other website.

  • Plain menu. Lessons, Monkey Wrench, Fitness, Podcast, Shop, Shoe Bet, About, Coming soon. No hole numbers. It's centered and set in the same bold type as the logo.
  • Plain labels. "Hole 5 · Par 4 · One-on-one" became "In person." "Hole 4 · Par 3 · Island green" became "Virtual lessons."
  • Real buttons at the bottom of each page instead of "Next tee → Hole 6."
  • The scrolling ticker is gone, and the typewriter-style font is used only for real information like prices and dates.
  • One golf joke stayed on purpose: "Par is a suggestion" in the footer.

Google's research on first impressions found that visually complex pages are rated less appealing, and simple, familiar layouts win. Every hole number and par label was one more thing a first-time visitor had to decode.

A home page that builds the Troop

You told me the podcast and the Shoe Bet are how Golf Monkey is building community buy-in right now. On the earlier design they sat near the bottom of the home page, three to four screens down on a phone. Most visitors never would have reached them.

FindingSourceWhat it means for Golf Monkey
57% of the time people spend on a page goes to the first screen, and 74% goes to the first two screens.Nielsen Norman Group (2018), via Williams CommerceWhatever sits in the first two screens is what most people see.
About 17% of viewing time lands just below the first screen.MixpanelThe spot right under the hero is the second-best place on the page.

So the podcast and the Shoe Bet now sit in a "Join the Troop" block directly under the hero, each with its own clip or photo and its own button. Underneath, one signup ties them together, with a nudge to tag @golfmonkeysc #shoebet.

The hero's two buttons are now "Listen to the podcast" and "Take the Shoe Bet." "Book a lesson" didn't go anywhere: it's pinned in the header on every page and it's the last thing on the home page. On phones, the hero is a little shorter, so the top of "Join the Troop" peeks above the fold. Seeing part of the next section is what tells people to keep scrolling.

The new home page order is: hero, Join the Troop, merch, "We're all just monkeys swinging a golf club," Where to start, and a closing "Book a lesson" band.

Why not a rotating hero?

I looked at putting the podcast and the Shoe Bet into a hero slideshow instead. The data on rotating heroes is consistent: almost nobody uses them, and the slides after the first barely get seen.

FindingSource
About 1% of visitors clicked a homepage carousel, and roughly 84% of those clicks went to the first slide.Erik Runyon, University of Notre Dame
A static image was clicked over 40% of the time, compared with 2% for a slider. Eye tracking showed people mostly ignored the slider.Conversionista, via Orbit Media
Removing a slider lifted one company's sales by 23%.Adobe client test, via Orbit Media

If the podcast and the Shoe Bet became slides two and three, roughly one visitor in ten would ever see them. Giving them their own cards at the top gets them close to first-slide attention without the downside.

Merch you can browse with a swipe

The new merch row, "Wear the Monkey," shows all eight products in a row people can swipe or scroll through, with a "Shop all merch" button. Each card links straight to that product in the Pro Shop. "Where to start" (Lessons, Monkey Wrench, Pro Shop and Fitness) works the same way on phones, and every card now has a photo.

That's a different thing from a rotating hero. Nothing moves on its own, the next card peeks in so people know there's more, and every card is the same kind of thing. That's how a product gallery works, and galleries test well: in one study, shoppers advanced product galleries 72% of the time (Mobify, via Orbit Media).

Phones get their own design

A lot of visitors will find Golf Monkey on their phone, often straight from Instagram, so the phone version was designed on its own instead of being a squeezed-down desktop page.

  • A real menu button that opens the full page list, with Tour and University under "Coming soon."
  • One column, full-width buttons, so nothing has to be pinched or zoomed.
  • Buttons at least 44 pixels tall, the size accessibility guidelines recommend so a thumb can hit them reliably (WCAG 2.1).
  • A "Back to top" button at the bottom of every page.
  • Same features on both. The desktop and phone home pages have the same sections in the same order. Only the layout changes.

I checked every page at phone, tablet, laptop and desktop widths. Nothing scrolls sideways, and on narrower laptops the menu folds into the menu button instead of wrapping onto two lines.

A note on color

The redesign keeps your colors: navy, cream and the Golf Monkey pink. I also tested a golf-themed version to see if a different palette would serve you better. The research pointed away from the obvious choice. Kelly green and yellow are so tied to the Masters that brands using them look like tribute merch, and golf apparel has been moving toward muted, timeless colors paired with classic navy (Sports Illustrated, Golf Digest). Azalea pink also turns out to be a classic golf accent (GolferHive), so your pink was never off-brand. The golf palette is there as an option if you want to see it.
